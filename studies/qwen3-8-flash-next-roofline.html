Roofline: Qwen3.8-Flash-Next
on 16 GB300s

Six billion activated parameters per token, 51 billion that cost no FLOPs at all, and a router that sends ten copies of every token across NVLink. This study turns the shapes from study 01 and the lifetimes from study 02 into an analytic ceiling for one recipe.

Anchor recipe, as in study 02: 16 GB300s in one NVLink domain, PP2 with interleaved 1F1B and two virtual stages, DP-shard8 / EP8, 16 microbatches of 16,384 packed tokens per DP rank in 4K documents, full layer recompute, Dist-MoE with slot factor 0.5, BF16 compute. The result is an optimistic analytic ceiling: FLOPs over practical compute rates, operand bytes over HBM, payload bytes over NVLink, plus the nominal pipeline bubble. Communication is priced byte by byte but assumed to overlap perfectly with compute, so it sets the step only if it exceeds compute. No measured throughput is used as an input or shown as a comparison.

Practical rates, not advertised peaks

Three roofs, and only one network

A GEMM is limited by its FLOPs or by the HBM bytes it must move. Distributed work is limited by the link that carries it. All 16 GPUs of the anchor sit in one NVLink domain, so expert dispatch, the PLE lookup, FSDP and pipeline packets all share the NVLink roof; no traffic reaches the scale-out network.

F = multiply-add FLOPs; B = operand bytes; intensity I = F / B.

The BF16 ridgeI* = P / βHBM = 225 FLOP/B

Below the ridge, fewer bytes help. Above it, the compute rate is the limit.

For every GEMM phase: forward, input gradient (DGRAD), weight gradient (WGRAD), and recompute replay
  t_op       = max(F_op / BF16_rate, operand_bytes / HBM_bandwidth)
T_stage      = microbatches * sum(t_op over local chunks) + attention_core_FLOPs / BF16_rate
T_nvl        = NVLink_bytes / NVLink_bandwidth
T_resource   = max over PP ranks of max(T_stage, T_nvl)        (assumes perfect overlap)
T_step       = T_resource + (PP - 1) / (M * VPP) * max(T_stage)
T_exposed    = max over PP ranks of (T_stage + T_nvl) + bubble  (no overlap at all)
rho          = global_tokens_per_update / (GPUs * T_step)
model TFLOP/s/GPU = rho * conventional_FLOPs_per_token / 1e12

Take 6ND apart

Stored parameters are not activated FLOPs

For Y = XW with X [M, K] and W [K, N], forward costs 2MNK FLOPs and backward adds dX = dY WT and dW = XT dY of the same size: 6MNK, or 6 Nactive FLOPs per token. Of 176.94B trainable parameters, only 6.67B are activated per token.

The table and the experts are opposite trades. The experts are 68% of the parameters and 35% of the activated weights. The table is 29% of the parameters and 0% of the FLOPs: it buys capacity with optimizer state (study 02) and network bytes, never with Tensor Core time.

Model FLOPs, issued work and MFU are different quantities. TorchTitan's TFLOPS and MFU use the conventional column: 6 × active parameters plus 6 × 24 × 512 × min(S, 2051) per QSA layer, the indexer term, and 18 × 48 × 128 × 128 per GDN layer. Recompute, the KL teacher and the attention backward's QK replay are real work but never raise MFU. More recompute can make a job look compute-bound while making it slower.
Follow each byte to its destination

The router moves more bytes than everything else together

Flash-Next dispatches the full 2,560-wide token, ten times, to experts on up to seven other GPUs. With uniform routing, a fraction 1 − 1/EP of the copies leaves the GPU. Each MoE layer sends them out (dispatch), back (combine), and reverses both in backward. Under full recompute, Dist-MoE re-dispatches the rows its slot did not keep. 

Expert payload / layer / microbatch = exchanges * T * 10 * (1 - 1/EP) * 2560 * 2 bytes
exchanges = 4 (dispatch, combine, and their reverses) + (1 - slot factor) under full recompute
          = 4.5 in the anchor: 16384 * 10 * 7/8 * 5120 * 4.5 = 3.08 GiB per layer per microbatch

PLE

IDs out, rows back

Each token asks for 16 rows. Without deduplication, 7/8 of them live on another owner: an 8-byte ID out, a 320-byte BF16 row back, and a 320-byte row gradient in backward. That is under 1% of the expert payload, and the 95-GiB table itself never crosses a link.

FSDP under PP

One gather, one reduction

PP keeps every local chunk gathered and defers gradient reduction to the end of the schedule, so dense weights cost one BF16 all-gather and one FP32 reduce-scatter per update: 6P(1 − 1/8) bytes. Experts have expert FSDP 1 and no collective at all. Without PP, each microbatch gathers twice and reduces once.

PP packets

The whole four-stream state

A stage boundary carries [16384, 4 × 2560] BF16, 320 MiB per microbatch forward and the same gradient backward. VPP2 puts three boundaries in every microbatch's path; each rank touches all three.

A budget, not a protocol trace. Payloads assume balanced routing, one copy per remote assignment and no metadata, alignment or routing-weight bytes. A dispatcher that sends a token once per destination rank and sums there would move fewer bytes. Competing collectives share one NVLink roof; overlap does not give each its own bandwidth.
Comms are priced, then assumed hidden. Assuming perfect overlap, the step is compute plus the bubble: NVLink service is 1.44 s per update on the pace-setting rank against 4.17 s of compute, 35% of it (40% on rank 0). That margin is what makes the assumption plausible, not a guarantee. Inside a MoE layer, dispatch, the expert GEMMs and combine form a dependency chain; only Dist-MoE's chunking and the other virtual stage's work can cover it. If nothing overlapped, the same bytes would stretch the step to 5.75 s and the ceiling to 967 model TFLOP/s/GPU, so the anchor's ceiling lies between 967 and 1,291 depending on how much of the expert traffic is hidden.
The useful FLOPs still need a schedule

The LM head makes the last stage the slow one

Compute per PP rank, one update
Nominal bubble overhead = (PP - 1) / (M * VPP)
Anchor: (2 - 1) / (16 * 2) = 3.13% of the slowest rank's compute
VPP1:   (2 - 1) / 16       = 6.25%

The bubble formula assumes balanced chunks. With the LM head and the final mixer on chunk 3, rank 1 has more work than rank 0 even though both own 24 decoder layers, and the faster rank waits. Moving the head's work, or giving rank 1 fewer decoder layers, would balance compute but shift memory: rank 0 already carries the table (study 02). Balancing one resource unbalances the other.

Once per update, not once per microbatch

Muon is a matrix workload of its own

DistMuon orthogonalizes each logical matrix with 8 Polar Express iterations on a BF16 working copy. For an expert matrix oriented as X [r, c] with r = 640 and c = 2560, an iteration forms A = XXT, a polynomial in A, and the update BX: 4r2c + 2r3 FLOPs. 


        

          The routed experts dominate: each GPU owns 24 layers × 64 experts × 3 matrices. The dense Muon matrices add little, and the
          narrow 640 side keeps every Gram matrix small. Muon is a small fraction of the ideal step, and more tokens per update amortize it further.
